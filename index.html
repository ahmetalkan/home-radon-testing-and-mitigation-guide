Home Radon Testing and Mitigation Guide: Closed-House Basics

Radon is a colorless, odorless radioactive gas formed by the natural decay of uranium in soil and rock beneath buildings. Because concentration varies dramatically from one house to the next—even on the same street—testing is the only way to know indoor levels.

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and the World Health Organization (WHO) both identify indoor radon exposure as a major preventable cause of lung cancer. EPA advises taking mitigation action when confirmed indoor radon reaches 4 picocuries per liter (pCi/L) or higher, and notes that homeowners may also consider mitigation for readings between 2 and 4 pCi/L.

Before you call a mitigator

National cost breakdowns, foundation selection guides, and monitor evaluations are compiled on RadonLab and its radon mitigation cost breakdown.

  • Test the lowest livable level: Place detectors in the lowest occupied area (such as a finished or regularly used basement), away from drafts, exterior walls, bathrooms, and sump pits.
  • Never rely on a zone map alone: EPA's Map of Radon Zones predicts regional geology averages, not individual house readings—high levels occur in Zone 3 counties as well.
  • Confirm credentials: Check whether your state licenses radon mitigators or recognizes national certification (NRPP / NRSB).
  • Require a post-mitigation test: Any finished system should be verified with an independent radon test at least 24 hours after the fan starts running.
Sealing floor cracks alone is rarely enough to lower elevated radon without active sub-slab depressurization, though sealing helps a mitigation fan draw efficiently.
